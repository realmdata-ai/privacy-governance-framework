# PGF-02 — Privacy Governance Charter

> **Legal uncertainty and business decision.** A disputed legal interpretation is recorded as a reasoned position with its assumptions, alternatives, consequences for the organisation, Legal/DPO advice and a review date. Uncertainty is not automatically a confirmed G-L failure. The designated Legal function records whether the proposed design has a supportable basis under the applicable rules; the authorised business role decides within those rules and its delegation. A confirmed missing prerequisite or prohibition remains a stop/redesign condition. The decision record presents benefits and consequences for individuals and the organisation together, without silently lowering the individual-harm score.

# About This Document

The Privacy Governance Charter is Document 2 of the Privacy Governance Framework (PGF). It sets the objectives, mandate, authority, and boundaries of the entire governance model, and is the parent document for the Interface & Integration Model (Document 3) and the Organisational Design Strategy (Document 4), while also framing the RACI Matrix (Document 5).

The Charter defines the role model used across the whole framework: the Controller, Executive Management, the Governing Body, the Executive Privacy Programme Owner (EPPO), the Processing Owner, the Data Protection Officer (DPO), the Privacy Operations Lead, the Privacy Risk Committee and Independent Internal Audit (Sections 8 and 9). The allocation of each process to these roles is fixed in the RACI Matrix (Document 5).

The template is designed for any large organisation operating across multiple jurisdictions. The GDPR is the legal baseline; the International Adaptation section sets out what changes under UK, Swiss and Canadian law.

# How to Use This Document

Part A is a drafting template. Bracketed text in bold italics marks a placeholder to be replaced with organisation-specific content. Boxes marked GUIDANCE explain what belongs in a section and are removed from the final, adopted Charter.

Part B shows the same structure fully completed for a fictional large retail bank. Names, addresses, and identifiers in Part B are fictional and for illustration only.

| GUIDANCE  Unlike the Privacy Policy (Document 1), the Charter is normally not a customer-facing document — it is an internal document for Executive Management, the Governing Body, governance committees and supervisory authorities, confirming that the privacy governance model has been formally established, has a mandate, and has approved boundaries of application. Some jurisdictions require publication of governance policies and practices (for example, Québec P-39.1 requires the person in charge to publish detailed information about governance policies and practices on the organisation's website); in that case publish the Charter or a faithful summary. |

## 2. What We Mean by Privacy

| GUIDANCE  This section fixes a working definition of the term “privacy” used across all documents of the model, to avoid conflating privacy with related but distinct concepts — information security, confidentiality, compliance. The Privacy Policy (Document 1) should use the same definition when it describes the organisation's approach to customers. |
| :---- |

In this model, privacy is understood as ***[the ability of a data subject to control the collection, use, and dissemination of information about themselves, together with a reasonable expectation that the organisation will process that information in a predictable manner, proportionate to its stated purposes]***.

Privacy and Related Concepts

* ***[Privacy ≠ information security — information security (confidentiality, integrity, availability of data) is one of the means of ensuring privacy, but is not equivalent to it: technically secured data can still be processed in a way that violates privacy (e.g., excessive collection or use beyond the stated purpose)]***

* ***[Privacy ≠ formal legal compliance — compliance is a necessary but not sufficient condition: processing may formally comply with the law while still failing to meet the data subject's reasonable expectations]***

* ***[Privacy ≠ a ban on data processing — privacy does not mean refraining from using data, but means using it lawfully and in a justified, proportionate, and accountable way (Section 7)]***

What Privacy Consists of Under This Model

* ***[Data subject control over their own data — exercised through data subject rights (Document 1, Section 12)]***

* ***[Predictability of processing — processing is limited to purposes the data subject was informed of]***

* ***[Proportionality of interference — the scope and manner of processing match its purpose and do not exceed what is necessary]***

* ***[Organisational accountability — the organisation is able to demonstrate compliance with these conditions through recorded evidence, not merely through statements]***

| *GUIDANCE  If the organisation uses privacy-enhancing technology, state here that its outputs are treated as evidence that supports human decisions, not as a guarantee of the absence of risk, and that only capabilities validated and recorded under the Privacy Automation & Tooling Strategy (Document 9, Section 8.1 — validation record) are relied on.* |
| :---- |

The Concept of Privacy Risk

| GUIDANCE  The definition of privacy risk should be aligned with the Privacy Risk Assessment Methodology (Document 18) — this section fixes the conceptual notion used across all documents of the model, not the calculation formula itself. |
| :---- |

In this model, privacy risk is understood as ***[the likelihood of adverse consequences for the rights and freedoms of individuals arising from the processing of personal data, together with the severity of those consequences should they occur]***. Consequences for the organisation (regulatory, financial, reputational) are assessed on a separate track and never offset risk to individuals (Section 7). Per the principle of the probabilistic nature of risk (Section 7), privacy risk is not a binary “yes/no” characteristic, but is determined by the combination of likelihood and severity of consequences.

What Privacy Risk Consists of

* ***[Risk source — a contextual description of a scenario that could lead to adverse consequences: unauthorised access to data, use of data outside its stated purpose, re-identification of de-identified data, an erroneous or discriminatory automated decision, loss of the data subject's control over their own data; the source is not a separately weighted factor in the calculation — it sets the context in which likelihood and severity are assessed]***

* ***[Likelihood of occurrence — assessed as an independent track under the Privacy Risk Assessment Methodology (Document 18), taking into account threats, the protective measures applied, third-party access, and the volume and sensitivity of the data; re-identification metrics are one input only where de-identified or synthetic data are released]***

* ***[Severity of consequences for individuals — assessed as an independent track under the Privacy Risk Assessment Methodology (Document 18), independent of the likelihood track, by type of harm: unlawful processing or loss of control, discrimination or adverse automated decisions, financial loss, identity fraud, reputational and social harm, physical or psychological harm, chilling effects and exclusion, loss of confidentiality, integrity or availability]***

* ***[Organisational consequences — regulatory, reputational and financial consequences for the organisation, assessed on a separate track (Document 18) and reported separately]***

* ***[Residual risk — the level of risk remaining after protective measures have been applied (acting on the data, the processing method, organisational measures — Section 7); it is the residual risk to individuals that is compared against the tiers (Document 8) when deciding who may accept it, after the legal compliance gate (G-L, Section 11) has been passed]***

| *GUIDANCE  This section describes risk through source (a contextual description of the scenario), likelihood, and severity. The Privacy Risk Assessment Methodology (Document 18) assesses likelihood and severity as two independent tracks, plotted against the Privacy Risk Appetite Statement matrix (Document 8); the source is not a separately weighted factor and should not be described as one — the wording here and Document 18's calculation model must not diverge.* |

Who Governs

The bodies and roles that govern are:

* ***[Governing Body and its Risk / Audit Committee — the Oversight tier, overseeing the programme and receiving assurance; reserved consent matters only where its rules so provide]***

* ***[Processing Owners in business units — taking processing decisions within delegated authority, per the RACI Matrix, Document 5]***

* ***[Independent Internal Audit — the third line, providing independent assurance to the Governing Body's Audit Committee]***

* ***[Other employees of the organisation who process personal data in the course of their duties — required to comply with the model's policies and standards, but not governance bodies]***

## 7. Principles of Privacy Governance

| GUIDANCE  Principles of privacy governance articulate how the organisation governs the privacy function itself. They are distinct from the data-processing principles of GDPR Art. 5, which bind every processing activity and are stated below so that the Charter is self-contained. The Privacy Policy (Document 1) applies the Art. 5 principles to the audience of each notice; it does not redefine them. |
| :---- |

Data-Processing Principles (GDPR Art. 5)

***[Organisation Name]*** processes personal data in accordance with the principles of GDPR Art. 5(1) — lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality — and is responsible for, and able to demonstrate, compliance with them (accountability, Art. 5(2)). No principle of privacy governance below may be applied in a way that weakens these principles.

Principles of Privacy Governance

* ***[Lawfulness first — every processing decision passes the legal compliance gate (G-L, Section 11) before any risk decision is taken; a G-L failure means stop or redesign, and no risk appetite, risk acceptance, DPO opinion or metric can cure it]***

* ***[Probabilistic nature of risk — privacy risk is not binary ("yes/no"): any decision to process data carries a non-zero likelihood of adverse consequences of varying severity for individuals, which must be assessed rather than declared "safe/unsafe"]***

* ***[Reducibility of risk — risk can be reduced by acting on three groups of factors: the data itself (minimisation, pseudonymisation, anonymisation techniques, synthetic data), the manner of processing (choice of technology, access restriction, environment segregation), and organisational measures (training, controls, segregation of duties); the model's task is to select a proportionate combination of these measures, not to eliminate risk entirely]***

* ***[Derived-data risk governance — de-identification, pseudonymisation, and synthetic data reduce but do not eliminate risk; pseudonymised data remain personal data, and whether information is anonymous is a contextual legal conclusion reached on documented evidence and re-assessed periodically (Document 18). Derived data remain subject to this model's governance until such a conclusion has been reached and recorded]***

* ***[Two-track risk — risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals and organisational consequences (regulatory, financial, reputational) are assessed on separate tracks; organisational benefit or consequence never offsets risk to individuals]***

* ***[Weighing risk against benefit within the law — for lawful processing, the residual risk to individuals is weighed against the benefits of the processing for the individuals concerned and for the organisation when choosing between lawful options; benefit is never used to justify processing that fails G-L or residual high risk that has not passed prior consultation (G-36)]***

* ***[Risk limit management — the organisation sets residual-risk tiers and ceilings for individual processes, data categories, and processing types (Document 8); each ceiling is a hard limit for that processing; aggregation of limits is used only for portfolio monitoring and reporting, never to offset a higher risk in one processing against a lower risk in another]***

* ***[Risk-based approach — the strictness of controls and the level of the decision-maker depend on the assessed residual risk, within the boundaries set by law]***

* ***[Proportionality — the strictness of controls matches the sensitivity of the data and the severity of consequences for individuals]***

* ***[Technological neutrality — the model does not depend on any specific automation tooling; whatever tooling is selected must support the adopted governance model — the gates, risk calculation, the limit system, the compilation of evidence packs, and the allocation of decision rights — rather than redefining the model to fit its own technical constraints]***

* ***[Single source of truth — each fact is recorded once, in a single authoritative document or register; views, extracts and linked evidence records are allowed, and independent statutory records required by law (for example, the Art. 33(5) breach log) are always kept]***

* ***[Evidence over assertion — compliance is confirmed by recorded artifacts, not by statements]***

* ***[Separation of risk calculation, independent advice and risk acceptance — the residual risk of a processing activity is calculated by the privacy operations function under the methodology (Document 18); the DPO gives independent advice on it; the acceptance decision is taken by the Processing Owner or, above the delegated tier, by Executive Management (Section 8); no single role combines calculation and acceptance, and the DPO never accepts risk]***

* ***[Privacy by design without dictating the engineering process — the implementation mechanism is determined by engineering teams, subject to the mandatory gates (Section 11)]***

* ***[No duplicate governance structures — the model is embedded into existing corporate structures rather than creating parallel ones]***

* ***[Independence of the DPO — the DPO reports directly to the highest management level, receives no instructions regarding the exercise of its tasks, is not dismissed or penalised for performing them, and holds no role that determines purposes or means of processing (Section 8)]***

* ***[Continuous improvement — the model is reviewed on the terms set by the Policy Review Cycle (Document 29), and does not remain static after adoption; this principle is the sole source of this rule for all documents of the model, including privacy governance processes (Section 11)]***

## 8. Roles, Mandates and Decision Rights

| GUIDANCE  The requirements for DPO independence must comply with applicable law (e.g., GDPR Arts. 37–39) and may not be weakened by internal decisions. Allocate each decision by its nature: who manages, who decides within delegation, who advises independently, who gives independent assurance. Do not move management decisions to the Governing Body, and do not make the DPO the programme's decision-maker. |
| :---- |
| ***GUIDANCE**  The Processing Owner role is not a new headcount position, but a mandate held by an existing business-line head. Do not confuse it with the "Process Owner" (Document 25) — the register of parties Accountable for privacy governance processes, generated as a projection of the RACI matrix: these are different roles with similar-sounding names. The Processing Owner role profile is set out in the Privacy Role Competency Framework (Document 6, Section 4.6); the register of current mandate holders is Document 25, Section 10.* |

Controller, Executive Management and Governing Body

* ***[The controller is [Organisation Legal Name]. It is ultimately accountable for compliance (GDPR Arts. 5(2), 24) and acts through Executive Management and the delegations recorded in this Charter and the RACI Matrix (Document 5)]***

* ***[Executive Management adopts this Charter and the privacy policies, designates the EPPO, the DPO and the Processing Owners, sets the delegated risk-acceptance tiers, accepts residual risk above those tiers, and takes the controller's decisions on prior consultation (Art. 36) and on breach notification (Arts. 33–34) through one designated member; every decision record names a single effective decider]***

* ***[The Governing Body oversees the programme through its [Risk / Audit] Committee: it receives the EPPO's programme reports, the DPO's reports where the DPO considers it necessary, and the independent assurance opinion of Internal Audit. It may hold reserved consent matters (for example, adoption of this Charter) where its rules so provide. It does not take operational decisions or accept operational risk]***

Executive Privacy Programme Owner (EPPO) Mandate

* ***[The EPPO is [title of the designated executive]. No separate new post is required; the mandate may be combined with an existing executive role, but not with the DPO role]***

* ***[The EPPO is accountable for operating the programme: drafting and maintaining policies for adoption by Executive Management, running the governing, core and supporting processes, metrics, training, the privacy risk register and programme reporting]***

* ***[The EPPO gives concurrence to Medium-tier risk acceptance by Processing Owners and prepares, with the Processing Owner, any prior consultation with the competent SA]***

Processing Owner Mandate

* ***[This Charter establishes the Processing Owner role — held by an existing business-line head (not a new staff position) and designated by Executive Management (never self-appointed) — with the following mandate:]***

* ***[Taking authorised management decisions about the processing activities of their business line within the delegation granted by the controller: launching or changing processing, owning the DPIA for that processing, and accepting residual risk up to the delegated tier (Low; Medium with EPPO concurrence) — detailed rules by tier in Document 8, Section 5, and Document 5, Section 7]***

* ***[Proposing, but not deciding, acceptance of High residual risk, which is decided by one named member of Executive Management (or by the Privacy Risk Committee where a written delegation designates it), after G-36 has been evaluated and, where the Art. 36 test is met, after prior consultation with the competent SA; Critical residual risk is not acceptable — stop or redesign]***

* ***[Conflict of interest is managed, not absent — the Processing Owner decides for their own business line's initiatives; the compensating controls are the legal compliance gate (G-L), the DPO's mandatory advice on every DPIA, the recorded reasons for any departure from that advice, the EPPO's concurrence at Medium, and escalation of High risk to Executive Management]***

DPO Mandate

***[Organisation Name]*** designates a Data Protection Officer (DPO) with the following mandate:

* ***[Designation and contact — the DPO is designated by Executive Management on the basis of professional qualities and expert knowledge (Art. 37(5)); the DPO's contact details are published and communicated to the competent SA (Art. 37(7)). The DPO may be a staff member or fulfil the tasks under a service contract (Art. 37(6)); a group may designate a single DPO if the DPO is easily accessible from each establishment (Art. 37(2))]***

Privacy governance at ***[Organisation Name]*** is implemented in practice by embedding privacy governance business processes into the organisation's overall business process model. Privacy governance processes do not form an isolated loop: they are part of the organisation's unified process map, grouped into three process groups — governing (G1–G9), core (C1–C5) and supporting (S1–S9) — and have explicit interface points with other corporate processes. Mandatory Gates

Every process that leads to a processing, risk or transfer decision applies the following gates, in this order where relevant. Their wording is identical across the framework; the RACI Matrix (Document 5, Section 5) assigns their owners.

* **G-L — Legal compliance gate.** Runs **before** any risk decision. Checks: lawful basis (Art. 6); condition for special-category data (Art. 9) or criminal-offence data (Art. 10); purpose compatibility (Art. 6(4)); transparency deliverable (Arts. 13/14); Art. 22 permissibility and safeguards; valid transfer tool and TIA outcome (Chapter V — the TIA where the mechanism requires one, see G-TIA); mandatory deadlines and statutory duties. **Failure means stop or redesign.** No risk appetite, risk acceptance, DPO opinion or metric can cure a G-L failure.
* **G-DPIA — DPIA triage.** Triggers: Art. 35(1); the Art. 35(3) cases; the SA lists under Art. 35(4); the **nine** WP248 rev.01 criteria (two or more → presumption that a DPIA is required). DPO advice is required on **every** DPIA (Art. 35(2)), whatever the score.
* **G-36 — Prior consultation.** G-36 is **evaluated** for every DPIA. It is a statutory test, independent of internal tiers.
  * **Trigger.** If the DPIA indicates that processing would still result in a **high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons** in the absence of further mitigating measures, the controller consults the competent SA **before processing starts** (Art. 36(1)). This applies irrespective of the internal tier.
  * **Internal tiers as an indicator.** An internal "High" or "Critical" residual tier is a strong indicator that the test is met. The legal conclusion is recorded separately, with reasons (Legal, with DPO advice).
  * **Stricter internal policy.** An organisation may adopt a stricter *internal policy* — e.g. "all residual High is routed to consultation". It must be labelled as internal policy, not as a GDPR obligation.
  * **During and after consultation.** Under Art. 36(2) the SA may give written advice within the statutory period, which may be extended; request information, which suspends the period; or use its Art. 58 powers, including a temporary or definitive limitation or ban.
  * **No authorisation by elapse.** An elapsed period, or the absence of SA comments, is **not** an authorisation or a certificate of lawfulness. G-L remains in force throughout, and the controller remains responsible.
* **G-TIA — Transfer equivalence, branched by mechanism.**
  * **Art. 45 adequacy.** Check that the decision covers the destination, sector and recipient (scope and eligibility, e.g. DPF certification), that it remains valid, and monitor it. There is no statutory obligation to redo the Commission's assessment. A stricter internal review is labelled as internal policy.
  * **Art. 46 appropriate safeguards (e.g. SCCs, BCRs).** A TIA plus supplementary measures (EDPB Recommendations 01/2020 v2.0, Step 2 and following).
  * **Art. 49 derogations.** Exceptional and interpreted restrictively. The specific conditions are documented.
  * G-L and DPIA duties apply under every mechanism.
  * The TIA under Art. 46 assesses the **laws and practices** of the destination: public-authority access, enforceable rights and redress, supplementary measures, and re-evaluation (CJEU C-311/18).
  * If essentially equivalent protection cannot be ensured, the transfer does not start or is suspended. Risk appetite cannot override this.
  * PETs, TEEs, homomorphic encryption and remote access are **not** automatic exemptions from Chapter V (EDPB Guidelines 05/2021).
  * A DPIA and Art. 36 may be needed in parallel with a TIA.
* **Statutory deadlines.** A missed statutory deadline is a breach of obligation and is escalated, even if an aggregate KPI stays green. Metrics state the denominator, how zero cases are handled, and the cut-off rule.

## 2. What We Mean by Privacy

PanEuropean takes the view that privacy is a person's ability to control the collection, use, and dissemination of information about themselves, together with a reasonable expectation that the bank will process that information in a predictable manner, proportionate to its stated purposes — primarily the purposes of opening and servicing an account, lending, and fraud prevention.

Privacy and Related Concepts

* Privacy ≠ information security — the encryption and access controls applied by PanEuropean (Section 13 of the Privacy Policy) are a means of ensuring privacy, but do not by themselves guarantee it: technically secured data about a customer's income can still be used for purposes beyond what the customer reasonably expected when opening the account

* Privacy ≠ formal GDPR compliance — the formal existence of a lawful basis for automated credit scoring does not remove PanEuropean's obligation to give the customer a genuine ability to obtain human intervention, express their point of view and contest the decision (Art. 22(3))

* Privacy ≠ a ban on data processing — PanEuropean does not refrain from using customer data in credit scoring and anti-fraud analytics, but ensures that such use is lawful, justified, proportionate, and accountable

What Privacy Consists of Under This Model

* Customer control over their own data — exercised through data subject rights and the "Request a Review" function for automated credit decisions (Document 1, Sections 8 and 12)

* Predictability of processing — customer data is used only for purposes disclosed in the Privacy Policy at account opening; a new purpose is introduced only after a purpose-compatibility check (Art. 6(4)) or on a new lawful basis, and with customer notice

* Proportionality of interference — for example, health data is requested only when applying for payment protection insurance, under an Art. 9(2) condition, and is not used for other purposes, including general credit scoring

* PanEuropean's accountability — every residual-risk decision above Low is confirmed by an evidence pack, available to the HBDI as lead SA on request

This governance model's risk-assessment methodology and data-transformation approach are defined independently of any specific tooling (Section 7). PanEuropean implements parts of them on the Bank's privacy engineering platform (PEP, illustrative). PEP outputs are treated as evidence that supports human decisions, not as a guarantee of the absence of risk; the capabilities relied on are those validated and recorded under the Privacy Automation & Tooling Strategy (Document 9, Section 8.1 — validation record).

The Concept of Privacy Risk

PanEuropean takes the view that privacy risk is the likelihood of adverse consequences for customers and other individuals arising from the processing of personal data, together with the severity of those consequences should they occur. Organisational consequences for the bank (regulatory, financial, reputational) are assessed on a separate track and never offset risk to individuals. For example, privacy risk in credit scoring is determined not by whether the bank uses a customer's income data as such, but by the likelihood of an erroneous or unjustifiably discriminatory decision and the severity of the consequences of such a decision for the customer concerned.

What Privacy Risk Consists of

* Risk source — for example: unauthorised access to customer data during remote technical support from India (Example Cloud Services Pvt Ltd, fictional), use of health data (payment protection insurance) for general credit-scoring purposes, re-identification of a customer in a pseudonymised dataset used to develop a scoring model, an erroneous automated decision to decline credit — the context in which likelihood and severity are assessed; the source itself is not included in the calculation as a separate factor

* Likelihood of occurrence — assessed taking into account threats, the protective measures applied (encryption, pseudonymisation, access control), the volume and sensitivity of the data involved, and the number of people with access — an independent track under the Privacy Risk Assessment Methodology

* Severity of consequences for individuals — for example: financial harm (an unwarranted credit decline), discrimination, reputational harm (disclosure of financial-position data), loss of autonomy (inability to contest the automated decision) — an independent track, independent of likelihood

* Organisational consequences (separate track) — regulatory sanctions, compensation and litigation costs, reputational damage to PanEuropean

* Residual risk — the level of risk to individuals after PET techniques and organisational measures have been applied; it is the residual risk that is compared against the tiers of PanEuropean's Privacy Risk Appetite Statement when deciding whether to launch a process or product, after the legal compliance gate has been passed

## 3. Background and Context of the Programme

Regulatory Context

* Tightening enforcement practice by data protection supervisory authorities regarding banks that use automated decision-making in lending; the EU AI Act classifies creditworthiness assessment as high-risk (Annex III), with the related high-risk obligations applying from 2 December 2027 under the AI Act as amended by the AI Omnibus — GDPR duties, including Art. 22, apply already

* No previously identified material breaches; anticipated supervisory reviews — a data protection audit by the HBDI and the financial supervisor's review of ICT risk management under DORA — require demonstrating a formalised and auditable privacy governance model

Business Context

* PanEuropean's expansion from a single German licence to a network of branches in four additional EU countries over the past three years under passporting, which has multiplied the jurisdictional complexity of data processing

* Migration of analytics and credit scoring onto the Bank's privacy engineering platform (PEP, illustrative), which supports — but does not define — the risk-assessment methodology and limit system set by this Charter independently of tooling (Section 7)

Risk Context

* A 40% increase in the volume of data subject requests (DSRs) over the past two years amid the expansion of digital service channels

* Heightened sensitivity among retail customers to the use of personal data in automated credit scoring, reflected in industry research on consumer trust in banks

Baseline State and Rationale for the Programme

Prior to the adoption of this Charter, privacy governance at PanEuropean was organised at the level of individual branches, with local practices not consolidated into a unified model: the processing register was maintained separately by country, risk assessment lacked a single methodology, and accountability for cross-border transfer decisions was not formalised at group level. Programme-management tasks had also accumulated with the Group DPO, which the 2026 review identified as incompatible with the DPO's advisory and monitoring role. This Charter establishes a single, auditable, bank-wide privacy governance model that consolidates these practices, separates programme ownership from the DPO function, and is commensurate with the scale and risk profile of PanEuropean as a pan-European retail bank.

## 7. Principles of Privacy Governance

Data-Processing Principles (GDPR Art. 5)

PanEuropean processes personal data in accordance with the principles of GDPR Art. 5(1) — lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality — and is responsible for, and able to demonstrate, compliance with them (Art. 5(2)). The Privacy Policy (Document 1) applies these principles to customers.

Principles of Privacy Governance

* Lawfulness first — for example, a proposal to include payment-protection-insurance health data in general credit scoring fails G-L (no Art. 9(2) condition for that purpose) and is stopped, whatever its calculated residual risk

* Probabilistic nature of risk — PanEuropean takes the view that privacy risk is not binary: for example, exchanging data with EuroCredit Bureau is not simply a "safe" or "unsafe" operation, but is characterised by an assessed likelihood and severity of possible consequences for customers, re-assessed whenever the process or data volume changes

* Reducibility of risk — PanEuropean reduces the likelihood of adverse consequences through three groups of measures: transforming the data itself (pseudonymising customer identifiers before transfer to PEP analytics environments, using synthetic data when developing scoring models), choosing the manner of processing (restricting access to special categories of data, segregating development and production environments), and organisational measures (mandatory training for Credit Decisions staff, segregation of duties between the initiator and the approver of a decision)

* Derived-data risk governance — pseudonymised transaction data remain personal data; the synthetic data PanEuropean uses for analytics and scoring-model training reduce but do not eliminate the risk of customer re-identification; derived data remain subject to this model's governance until a documented determination under Document 18 concludes that the information is anonymous, and that determination is re-assessed periodically

* Two-track risk — the benefit to the bank of faster credit processing is recorded on the organisational track and is never used to lower the assessed risk to customers

* Weighing risk against benefit within the law — for example, when choosing between two lawful designs of automated credit scoring with comparable residual risk, the option with greater benefit to the customer (a faster decision with a working human-review route) is preferred; benefit is never used to justify a G-L failure or to avoid prior consultation

* Risk limit management — PanEuropean sets residual-risk ceilings separately for credit-scoring processes, cross-border transfers, the processing of special categories of data (payment protection insurance), and marketing; each ceiling is a hard limit for that processing; the aggregate view of all ceilings, adopted by the Management Board, is used only for portfolio monitoring and reporting to the Risk Committee of the Supervisory Board, never to offset risk between processing activities

* Risk-based approach — the level of the decision-maker depends on the assessed residual risk and the tiers adopted by the Management Board (Section 8)

* Proportionality — health data (payment protection insurance) and biometric data are subject to an enhanced control regime under the Data Classification Policy

* Technological neutrality — PanEuropean's privacy governance model does not depend on any specific automation tooling; the gates, the risk-assessment methodology, the limit system, and the allocation of decision rights are defined by this Charter and the Privacy Risk Assessment Methodology independently of tooling. PanEuropean implements parts of this model on PEP (risk calculation, PET techniques, compilation of evidence packs) only to the extent that the relevant functions have been validated and recorded under Document 9

* Single source of truth — the distribution of accountability is maintained exclusively in the Privacy RACI Matrix; the Process Owner Register is a derivative of it; the breach register required by Art. 33(5) is kept as an independent statutory record

* Evidence over assertion — every above-Low residual-risk decision is recorded as an evidence pack, available to the HBDI on request

* Separation of risk calculation, independent advice and risk acceptance — residual risk is calculated by the Privacy Operations Lead's team under Document 18; the Group DPO advises; the Processing Owner (Low, Medium with the Chief Privacy Officer's concurrence) or the CRO (High, after G-36 has been evaluated and, where the Art. 36 test is met, after prior consultation with the HBDI) decides; no role at PanEuropean combines calculation and acceptance

* Privacy by design without dictating the engineering process — the hard requirements for product and engineering teams are G-L and DPIA triage before going into production, and a completed DPIA where triage requires one

* No duplicate governance structures — the Privacy Risk Register is aligned with PanEuropean's corporate risk register through the Interface & Integration Model

* Independence of the DPO — the Group DPO reports directly to the Management Board, has direct access to the chairs of the Risk Committee and the Audit Committee of the Supervisory Board, and holds no role that determines purposes or means of processing

* Continuous improvement — the model is reviewed on the terms set by the Policy Review Cycle, and does not remain static after adoption; in particular, at least once a year and upon every material regulatory change in the countries of operation. This principle is the sole source of this rule for all documents of PanEuropean's model, including privacy governance processes (Section 11)

## 8. Roles, Mandates and Decision Rights

Controller, Management Board and Supervisory Board

* The controller is PanEuropean Bank AG. The Management Board adopts this Charter and the privacy policies, designates the Chief Privacy Officer, the Group DPO and the Processing Owners, sets the delegated risk-acceptance tiers, and takes the controller's decisions above those tiers. The CRO is the Management Board member responsible for the privacy programme; the CRO decides on High residual risk (unless a written delegation assigns a class of decisions to the Privacy Risk Committee), on prior consultation (Art. 36) and on breach notification (Arts. 33–34), with the COO as deputy; each decision record names one decider

* The Supervisory Board supervises the Management Board (AktG §111). Management measures are not transferred to it. Its Risk Committee receives the Chief Privacy Officer's quarterly programme report, is informed of every High-tier acceptance, every prior consultation and every notifiable breach, and receives the Group DPO's reports where the Group DPO considers it necessary; its Audit Committee receives Group Internal Audit's assurance opinion. Under its rules of procedure (AktG §111(4)), exactly three privacy matters require its consent: (1) the adoption and material amendment of this Charter; (2) the adoption and material amendment of the board-level privacy risk appetite (Privacy Risk Appetite Statement, Section 3); (3) the removal of the Group DPO. No other privacy matter is reserved

Chief Privacy Officer (EPPO) Mandate

* The Chief Privacy Officer is PanEuropean's EPPO and reports to the CRO. The Chief Privacy Officer is accountable for operating the programme: drafting and maintaining policies for adoption by the Management Board, running the governing, core and supporting processes, metrics, training, the Privacy Risk Register and programme reporting; gives concurrence to Medium-tier risk acceptance; and prepares, with the Processing Owner, any prior consultation with the HBDI

Processing Owner Mandate

* This Charter establishes the Processing Owner role — held by an existing PanEuropean head (Credit Decisions, Digital Banking, Marketing, Customer Service, Fraud Prevention, Insurance Distribution, Compliance (AML screening), Payment Services, Human Resources, Branch Network; the CIO for IT-operated processing; the CPO for the privacy function's own processing — holders in Document 25, Section 10), designated by the Management Board on the Chief Privacy Officer's proposal, not a new staff position — with the following mandate:

* Taking processing decisions for the line's data-processing activities within delegated authority: launching or changing processing, owning the DPIA for that processing, and accepting residual risk at the Low tier and, with the Chief Privacy Officer's concurrence, at the Medium tier (with the CRO's concurrence where the Chief Privacy Officer is itself the Processing Owner — Document 8, Section 5)

* Proposing High-tier acceptance to the CRO (or to the Privacy Risk Committee where a written delegation designates it); Critical residual risk is not acceptable — the initiative is stopped or redesigned

* Conflict of interest is managed, not absent — the Processing Owner decides for their own business line's initiatives; the compensating controls are G-L, the Group DPO's mandatory advice on every DPIA, recorded reasons for any departure from that advice, the Chief Privacy Officer's concurrence at Medium, and escalation of High risk to the CRO

Group DPO Mandate

* Designation — the Management Board of PanEuropean Bank AG has designated the Group DPO as the bank's DPO; the Group DPO is employed by PanEuropean Bank AG, is also designated by other group entities under Art. 37(2), and is easily accessible from each establishment, supported by a Deputy Group DPO and local-language contact channels in each country of operation. The contact details are published in the Privacy Policy and communicated to the HBDI and, where national law so requires, to the concerned SAs

* Statutory tasks — informing and advising the bank and its employees; monitoring compliance with the GDPR, the BDSG and national law in all countries of operation, including the compliance audits the Group DPO organises (Art. 39(1)(b)); advising on and monitoring every DPIA; cooperating with and acting as the contact point for the HBDI and the concerned SAs; acting as the contact point for customers and other data subjects

* Direct access to the highest management level — the Group DPO reports directly to the Management Board (administratively through its Chair) and has an unrestricted right of access to the chairs of the Risk Committee and the Audit Committee of the Supervisory Board, in addition to — not instead of — the reporting line to the Management Board

* Independence and resources — the Group DPO receives no instructions on the exercise of its tasks, has its own budget line for expert knowledge and certification upkeep (Art. 38(2)), and is protected against dismissal and penalties for performing its tasks (Art. 38(3)); as a mandatory DPO in Germany, the special dismissal protection of §38(2) with §6(4) BDSG applies, which the CJEU confirmed Member States may provide (C-453/21 X-FAB)

* Confidentiality — the Group DPO is bound by secrecy or confidentiality (Art. 38(5)); customer complaints and staff reports addressed to the Group DPO are shared with the Chief Privacy Officer, the Privacy Operations Lead or a business line only on a need-to-know basis decided by the Group DPO, and are otherwise reported in aggregate or redacted form; this does not prevent the Group DPO from investigating a matter or discussing it with management

* No conflict of interests — the Group DPO holds no position that determines purposes or means of processing, does not report to the CDO, the CISO or any business-line head, and the conflict assessment is recorded on designation and reviewed annually

* What the Group DPO does not do — the Group DPO does not approve residual risk, block launches, decide on breach notification, or determine whether data are anonymous or whether an operation is a Chapter V transfer; the anonymity conclusion is taken by the responsible Processing Owner on General Counsel's documented legal assessment, supported by the PET Assessment Module (Document 18), and the transfer determination is recorded by General Counsel at the G-L gate (Documents 12 and 20) — in both cases on the Group DPO's advice

## 11. Privacy Governance Processes

Privacy governance at PanEuropean Bank AG is implemented in practice by embedding privacy governance business processes into the bank's overall business process model. Privacy governance processes do not form an isolated loop: they are integrated into PanEuropean's unified process map, grouped into the governing (G1–G9), core (C1–C5) and supporting (S1–S9) process groups, and explicitly interface with credit risk, information security, ICT risk and compliance processes. The complete process model is set out in the Privacy Governance Process Map (Document 24); roles are allocated in PanEuropean's Privacy RACI Matrix (Document 5).

Every PanEuropean process that leads to a processing, risk or transfer decision applies the mandatory gates G-L, G-DPIA, G-36 and G-TIA exactly as worded in Part A, Section 11. At PanEuropean, General Counsel owns G-L and the G-TIA determination, the relevant Processing Owner owns the DPIA, the Group DPO advises on every DPIA, and the CRO decides on any prior consultation with the HBDI. For example, the credit-scoring case passes G-L only on the basis of contract necessity under Art. 22(2)(a) with the Art. 22(3) safeguards (human intervention, the right to express a point of view and to contest the decision), as recorded in `DPIA-CS-LAUNCH-01`.

This declaration is an application of the continuous-improvement principle (Section 7) to PanEuropean's privacy governance processes: the bank does not treat processes as static after initial adoption, but systematically reviews and improves them based on the Group DPO's monitoring results, Group Internal Audit findings, feedback from the business lines, and growth in transaction volumes. The cadence and grounds for review are established exclusively by the Policy Review Cycle (Document 29) and are not redefined by this section.

This Charter records PanEuropean's process model at the model level, not at the operational level: the operational decomposition of processes — the subject matter of the Privacy Governance Process Map (Document 24) — evolves independently and does not require this Charter to be revised. Grounds for an unscheduled revision of this Charter are not any change to Document 24, but only a structural change — the emergence of a new process group, a change to the mandatory gates, or a change in what is governed or who governs (Section 4) not covered by the current version of this Charter. The 2026 re-baselining of the role model was such a structural change and led to version 3.0.

PanEuropean has committed to automating and digitising its privacy governance processes on the Bank's privacy engineering platform (PEP, illustrative) in accordance with the Privacy Automation & Tooling Strategy: risk calculation, the compilation of evidence packs, monitoring of Privacy Risk Appetite tiers, and the application of PET techniques are moved onto PEP as the corresponding stages of technical integration are completed and validated. PEP supports the gates and decisions defined by this Charter; it does not take any decision that this Charter assigns to a role.

PanEuropean's process model is detailed in Document 24 down to the operational level, including the "Data Request and Transformation" process (C2). Further development of the process model is recorded directly in Document 24 under the procedure set out by the continuous-improvement principle (Section 7): such development does not affect the structure of the process groups and does not require this Charter to be revised, since it does not constitute a structural change within the meaning defined above.

# International Adaptation

> **Guidance.** The GDPR is the baseline of this Charter. The box below states what changes elsewhere. It does not claim compliance with any jurisdiction's law; the Regulatory Applicability Matrix (Document 7) states where each regime applies.

**United Kingdom (headline).** Under UK GDPR and the DPA 2018, as amended by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (phased commencement), the role model of Section 8 can be kept: the DPO provisions of UK GDPR Arts. 37–39 continue to apply, and the competent authority is the Information Commissioner's Office.

**Switzerland (headline).** Under the revFADP, appointing a data protection advisor is optional for private controllers (Art. 10). Where an advisor meets the statutory independence and expertise conditions, the controller may consult the advisor instead of the FDPIC after a DPIA showing high residual risk (Art. 23(4)); record that route in the G-36 gate for Swiss processing.

**Canada — PIPEDA and Québec.** Accountability is organised differently, and the Charter is adapted as follows:

* **Privacy Officer ≠ DPO.** PIPEDA Principle 4.1 requires the organisation to designate one or more individuals who are accountable for its compliance (4.1.1), to make their identity known on request (4.1.2), to remain responsible for personal information transferred to a third party for processing (4.1.3), and to implement policies and practices (4.1.4). Under Québec P-39.1, the person exercising the highest authority is the person in charge of the protection of personal information by default and may delegate that function in writing, in whole or in part (s. 3.1); the title and contact information are published on the website. These roles are **accountability** roles. In this Charter they normally map to the EPPO (with Executive Management for Québec, unless delegated), **not** to the EU DPO, whose independence restrictions do not transfer automatically.
* **Publication.** Québec requires governance policies and practices to be published in clear and simple terms (s. 3.2); publish this Charter or a faithful summary for Québec operations.
* **Gates.** G-L is applied against PIPEDA's meaningful-consent and appropriate-purposes requirements, not GDPR Art. 6 bases; G-36 has no direct PIPEDA equivalent; for Québec, a privacy impact assessment is required before personal information is communicated outside Québec and for projects involving information systems, and the breach track follows PIPEDA (real risk of significant harm; records of every breach of security safeguards for 24 months) and Québec (confidentiality incidents; register of all incidents) — not the GDPR 72-hour clock.
