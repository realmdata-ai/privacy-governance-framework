# PGF-13 — Consent Management Policy

*Privacy Governance Framework (PGF) · Level 2 — Policies and Standards · Release 1.0.0-rc.2 · Regulatory reference date: 26 October 2026*

> **Status and use.** Part A is a reusable template. Part B is a worked example for a fictional organisation (PanEuropean Bank AG); the named implementation profile identifies a real product; the Bank, deployment, figures, dates and records in Part B are illustrative. Nothing in this document is legal advice or a statement about any real organisation, product or provider.

# **About This Document**

**Purpose.** Document 13 sets the requirements for the form and content of consent, the procedure for recording, withdrawing and renewing it, the propagation of a withdrawal to processors and derived data, and the link between consent status and the permission to process — for the processing operations whose lawful basis is consent (Art. 6(1)(a) GDPR) or whose special-category condition is explicit consent (Art. 9(2)(a)).

**Scope.** Only processing recorded in the RoPA (Document 21) with consent as the lawful basis or as the Art. 9 condition. Consent under the ePrivacy rules for cookies and similar technologies and for electronic marketing is covered by a pointer (Section 2): the same standard of form and withdrawal applies, but the operational detail sits in the cookie notice and the national rules recorded in Document 7.

**Relationships.** Document 1 (Privacy Policy and Notice) — the table of purposes and lawful bases, which is the single source for where consent applies; Document 7 — national digital-consent ages and ePrivacy rules; Document 10 — special categories and the vulnerable-data-subject marker; Document 11 — retention of proof of consent; Document 15 — withdrawal outside the organisation's own channels and the right to object; Document 18 — consent is checked in the legal compliance gate (G-L), not scored as a risk factor; Document 5, Section 5.5 — roles in core process C5.

## **1. Purpose**

This document establishes the requirements for the form and content of consent, the procedure for recording, withdrawing and renewing it, and the link between consent status and the permission to process personal data in a specific process — for those processing operations whose lawful basis is consent or whose special-category condition is explicit consent.

## **2. Scope and Consent-Based Processing**

> **Guidance.** Consent is one of several lawful bases, not the default. This document does not govern processing for which another basis applies — even if the person has formally "agreed" to it, for example by accepting contract terms. Using consent where another basis applies is not a safer choice: consent must be freely given and can be withdrawn at any time, which is incompatible with processing that is necessary for a contract or required by law (for example, customer due diligence under anti-money-laundering law).

* ***[**Two standards.** Consent under Art. 6(1)(a) and explicit consent for special-category data under Art. 9(2)(a) (Document 10). Explicit consent requires an express statement of consent (Section 4). Where explicit consent is relied on for a decision based solely on automated processing (Art. 22(2)(c)) or for a transfer under Art. 49(1)(a), the same explicit standard and the additional information required for those cases apply.]***

* ***[**Children.** The digital-consent age under Art. 8 applies only where an information-society service is offered directly to a child and the basis is consent — for example, a child creating their own account in the app or consenting to marketing through their own access. The age is set by each Member State between 13 and 16 and is recorded per jurisdiction in Document 7; there may be no single age across the jurisdictions of presence. Below that age, consent is given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility, and the organisation makes reasonable efforts, taking into account available technology, to verify that authorisation (Art. 8(2)). Opening a product for a minor through a legal representative is governed by national civil law on legal capacity and by the contract, not by consent within the meaning of this document.]***

* ***[**Vulnerable data subjects.** This document sets the consent safeguards for data subjects carrying the vulnerable-data-subject marker of the Data Classification Policy (Document 10). Children are one example; record whether the organisation has others — for example, customers with limited legal capacity or cognitive impairments, or financially vulnerable customers — and the consent safeguards applied to them.]***

* ***[**Consent is not a risk factor.** Valid consent is checked in the legal compliance gate (G-L) as a lawful basis or Art. 9 condition (Document 18, Section 2.1). It does not reduce the severity or the likelihood of harm in the risk calculation (Document 18, Section 3.4), and a missing or invalid consent is a G-L failure — stop or redesign — not a factor to be scored and accepted. This document makes consent valid and provable; it is not a calculation input.]***

* ***[**Consent versus the ePrivacy "soft opt-in".** Consent within the meaning of this document is always given by a clear affirmative act (opt-in). The ePrivacy Directive (Art. 13(2)) allows electronic mail marketing of the organisation's own similar products to existing customers without prior consent, provided the customer was given a clear opportunity to object free of charge at collection and in every message; the GDPR basis for such processing is then legitimate interests (Art. 6(1)(f); Recital 47), with the absolute right to object to direct marketing (Art. 21(2)–(3), Document 15). National implementations differ materially — which channels are covered, how "similar" is read, and what must be said at collection — so the exception is checked per jurisdiction (Document 7) and is never assumed to be uniform. Whatever the basis, one withdrawal and opt-out mechanism (Section 6) applies, so the person does not need to know on which basis a communication was sent in order to stop it.]***

* ***[**Cookies and similar technologies.** Storing information on, or accessing information in, a user's terminal equipment requires consent unless it is strictly necessary for a service the user requested (ePrivacy Directive, Art. 5(3), as implemented nationally — Document 7; technical scope per EDPB Guidelines 2/2023, including pixels, software development kits and push tokens). The operational detail sits in the cookie notice and the consent tool, not in this document; the standards of form, granularity and withdrawal of Sections 4 and 6 apply to it. Where a cookie or SDK feeds further processing of personal data, that processing needs its own lawful basis recorded in the RoPA.]***

* ***[**Consent collected by others.** Consent collected by a third party or a joint controller on the organisation's behalf (for example, by a partner in a joint product) is admitted only where the arrangement with that party (Art. 26 arrangement or data-sharing agreement, Document 17) names the organisation as a recipient, gives the organisation access to the proof of consent and provides for the propagation of withdrawals. The counterparty is assessed under Document 17; the consent itself must meet this document.]***

The Processing Owner of each consent-based purpose decides on the design of the consent request and on the configuration of the consent management system for that purpose, with DPO advice; the privacy function sets the requirements of this document and owns the storage and proof of consent; IT operates the system (Document 3, Section 3.9; Document 5, Section 5.5).]***

## **4. Requirements for the Form and Content of Consent**

* ***[**Freely given (Art. 4(11), 7(4); Recitals 42–43).** Consent is not a condition of a contract or service that does not need the data; a product requiring consent (for example, optional insurance) is genuinely optional, and refusing it has no detriment beyond not obtaining that product. Imbalance of power is assessed: where the person cannot refuse without detriment, consent is not used.]***

* ***[**Specific and granular.** Consent is requested for a specific purpose; where there are several purposes that are not inextricably linked, consent is granular for each, so that the person can agree to one and decline another.]***

* ***[**Informed.** At least: the identity of the controller; the purpose of each processing operation for which consent is sought; what (type of) data will be collected and used; the existence of the right to withdraw consent; where relevant, the use of the data for automated decision-making under Art. 22(2)(c); and, where consent is the basis for a transfer under Art. 49(1)(a), the possible risks of the transfer (EDPB Guidelines 05/2020). The information is given in clear and plain language in the consent request itself, not by reference to documents that must be sought out.]***

* ***[**Unambiguous.** A statement or clear affirmative action: no pre-ticked boxes, no inactivity, no consent inferred from continued use. Interfaces do not use deceptive design patterns — for example, visual emphasis of "accept", repeated prompts after a refusal, or hiding the refusal option (EDPB Guidelines 03/2022).]***

* ***[**Explicit consent (Art. 9(2)(a), and Arts. 22(2)(c), 49(1)(a) where used).** An express statement of consent by the person — for example, a written or electronically signed statement, an electronic form completed by the person, or a two-stage verification in which the person confirms a specific statement naming the special category and the purpose (EDPB Guidelines 05/2020). The form is separate from other consents and from general terms. Record whether the organisation, as internal policy, excludes a single tick box even where the box carries an express statement; such a rule is stricter than the legal standard and is labelled as internal policy.]***

* ***[**Age.** Where consent may be given in an information-society service offered directly to children, the form establishes the person's age by means proportionate to the risk (for example, the verified date of birth held for the customer relationship) and, below the national age, obtains or verifies the authorisation of the holder of parental responsibility (Art. 8(2)).]***

* ***[**Change to the consent text.** Where the consent text changes materially (purpose, recipients or categories of recipients, data categories, or a new channel), consent collected under the earlier text does not cover the changed processing; renewed consent under the new text is obtained before the changed processing begins. Non-material changes (editorial amendments that do not affect the scope or purpose) do not require renewed consent but are recorded as a new version of the text (Section 5).]***

## **5. Procedure for Recording Consent**

***[Every consent is recorded in a single consent register (consent management system) stating: the data subject; the purpose; the version of the consent text and the information shown; the date, time and channel of collection; the means of age or authorisation check where relevant; and the status (active, withdrawn, expired). The register is the single authoritative source of consent status; the RoPA (Document 21) refers to it and does not duplicate it.]***

***[The register keeps an append-only history of status changes (obtained → withdrawn or expired → possibly obtained again), not a single mutable status field: the organisation must be able to demonstrate that consent was valid at the time it was relied on, including consent that has since been withdrawn or has expired (Art. 7(1)). How long the proof of consent is kept is set by the Data Retention and Disposal Policy (Document 11); the validity of consent is set by this document.]***

## **6. Withdrawal, Opt-Out and Propagation**

* ***[**As easy as giving consent (Art. 7(3)).** Withdrawal takes no more effort than giving consent: in the same channel, without additional steps, authentication beyond the standard log-in, or contact with support, unless these were also required to give consent. The person is told before giving consent that it can be withdrawn at any time.]***

* ***[**Act first, then inform.** A withdrawal takes effect immediately for future processing and is recorded at once. Where the withdrawal has consequences for a product (for example, an insurance application that cannot continue without health data), the organisation informs the person of those consequences after recording the withdrawal; it does not delay the withdrawal until the person has been informed. A withdrawal does not affect the lawfulness of processing carried out before it.]***

* ***[**Single point of control.** The withdrawal of consent and the objection to direct marketing based on legitimate interests are implemented through the same access point (preference centre) with the same ease of use. The right to object under Art. 21 is brought explicitly to the person's attention, clearly and separately from other information, at the latest at the time of the first communication (Art. 21(4)).]***

* ***[**Channels outside the organisation's own interfaces.** A withdrawal received by telephone, letter or e-mail is handled under the Data Subject Rights Policy (Document 15) — immediately, within the same interaction where possible — and recorded directly in the consent register, not in a separate list.]***

* ***[**Propagation to processors and recipients.** On withdrawal, the consent register pushes the new status to every system that uses it and to every processor processing the data for that purpose, which stops the processing and deletes or returns the data as its contract provides (Document 17). Where personal data were disclosed to another controller on the basis of the consent, the organisation informs that recipient of the withdrawal so far as the arrangement with it provides (Document 17).]***

* ***[**Propagation to derived data.** Where consent was the only basis for processing, the data are erased unless another basis applies to them (Art. 17(1)(b)). The erasure is propagated along the data lineage to derived personal data — profiles, segments, pseudonymised copies, feature stores and synthetic datasets for which no anonymity conclusion has been made — under the erasure cascade of Document 11, Section 3.5. Data that another basis requires to be kept (for example, proof of consent, or statutory records) enter the restricted state (Document 11, Section 4.1) and are not used for the withdrawn purpose.]***

## **7. Link Between Consent Status and the Permission to Process**

***[Before each use of personal data in a consent-based process, the current status is checked in the consent register — a step on the process map (Document 24, core process C5). Processing does not proceed where consent has been withdrawn or has expired, whether or not a local copy in an executing system has been synchronised. Executing systems do not keep their own copy of consent status longer than the single operation for which it was read. A withdrawal stops use and dispatch at once: queued or scheduled messages for the person are cancelled or blocked by a suppression check performed immediately before each dispatch (not only before a campaign starts), so that no message is sent after the withdrawal has been recorded; synchronisation cycles with processors may govern the later deletion of retained copies, but never the stopping of use. The same applies to an objection to direct marketing (Art. 21(3)).]***

## **8. Renewal and Expiry of Consent**

***[The GDPR sets no fixed expiry for consent; the EDPB recommends refreshing consent at appropriate intervals, depending on the context and the person's expectations (EDPB Guidelines 05/2020). Record the organisation's policy choice: whether consent for each purpose has a defined validity period, when renewal is offered, and what happens on expiry — in which case processing based on it stops without further notice. Record also what happens to consent when the customer relationship ends (for example, whether marketing to former customers continues, and for how long), taking into account reasonable expectations and national supervisory practice (Document 7).]***

## **1. Purpose**

This document sets PanEuropean Bank AG's consent requirements for the processing operations whose lawful basis is consent or whose special-category condition is explicit consent, across the five countries of presence (Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Poland).

## **2. Scope and Consent-Based Processing**

Consent is used narrowly at PanEuropean. Per the table of purposes and lawful bases in the Customer Privacy Notice (Document 1, Section 7): customer identification and anti-money-laundering checks — legal obligation; account, payments and cards — contract; creditworthiness assessment — legal obligation; fraud prevention, transaction monitoring and reporting to EuroCredit Bureau — legitimate interests, each with a legitimate interests assessment and, where triage requires, a DPIA. Consent is the basis, or the Art. 9 condition, only for the processing in the following list, which is the single list at PanEuropean:

| Consent-based processing | Lawful basis / Art. 9 condition | Processing Owner | Collection channel | Notes |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| E-mail and push marketing of PanEuropean products | Consent, Art. 6(1)(a); national electronic-marketing rules (Document 7) | Head of Marketing | App, online banking, branch | Granular by channel (e-mail; push) |
| Personalised offers in the app (pilot for a limited group of customers), based on contact details and product preferences recorded in the customer-relationship system | Consent, Art. 6(1)(a), given separately for this purpose | Head of Digital Banking | App | Document 18, Example 4.1: G-L confirmed; triage — one WP248 criterion, no DPIA; track (a) Low. Not extended to transaction data without a new assessment and an updated notice |
| Health data for payment protection insurance (PPI) arranged by PanEuropean as distributor for the insurer | Contract, Art. 6(1)(b), with explicit consent for health data, Art. 9(2)(a); Art. 9(2)(f) only where needed for legal claims | Head of Insurance Distribution | App, branch, contact centre — at loan origination or separately | The insurer is a separate controller for underwriting and claims. Health data are never used for creditworthiness assessment |
| Biometric log-in through the biometric module of PanEuropean's app | Explicit consent, Art. 9(2)(a) — freely given because a non-biometric log-in is always available | Head of Digital Banking | App (activation in settings) | Template generated and stored only on the customer's device (Document 10, Section 4.5). Log-in through the device operating system's own biometric function involves no biometric data at PanEuropean and is not consent-based processing under this document |

* **Not on the list.** Placement of offers through an external advertising partner was assessed (Document 18, Examples 4.2 and 4.2-R) but is not in operation at 26 October 2026; it would require its own specific consent and an updated Customer Privacy Notice before launch and would then be added to this list. PanEuropean does not currently rely on the ePrivacy soft opt-in exception: Legal & Compliance reviewed it country by country and found the conditions — the channels covered, the reading of "similar products" and what must be said when the address is collected — too divergent across the five countries for the single app; all e-mail and push marketing is therefore consent-based. The right to object to direct marketing (Art. 21(2)–(3)) applies in addition and is handled through the same control (Section 6).

* **Children.** The digital-consent ages in the five countries range from 14 to 16 (Document 7, Section 4.1); they apply only to information-society services offered on the basis of consent. As an internal design choice, the app's marketing and personalisation features and the consent requests for them are switched off for every user under 16 in all five countries (Document 7). PanEuropean therefore does not seek marketing or personalisation consent from children; accounts for minors are opened by their legal representatives under national civil law, which is a contractual matter outside this document.

* **Vulnerable data subjects.** Data of minor holders of children's banking products carry the vulnerable-data-subject marker (Document 10); the switch-off above is the consent safeguard for them. In addition, as internal policy, customers in a financial-hardship arrangement are not shown requests for consent to credit-product marketing and receive no credit-product offers while the arrangement lasts. No other group requiring a separate consent regime has been identified; the Privacy Operations Lead reviews this assessment annually with the Head of Marketing.

* **Consent and risk.** Consent is checked by Legal & Compliance in G-L (Document 18, Section 2.1). It is not a severity or likelihood factor in PanEuropean's risk calculations; in Document 18, Example 4.1 the personalised-offers pilot is Low on its own threat and harm factors, and consent is recorded only as the lawful basis that G-L confirmed.

* **Cookies and SDKs.** Consent to non-essential cookies and similar technologies on paneuropean-bank.example and in the app is governed by the Cookie Notice and the consent tool referred to in the Customer Privacy Notice (Document 1, Section 14), applying the national rules recorded in Document 7 (for example, TDDDG §25 in Germany). Sections 4 and 6 of this document apply to that tool's form and withdrawal standard.

* **Consent collected by others.** All consents in the list are collected directly by PanEuropean. The PPI insurer collects any consent it needs for its own underwriting and claims under its own notice. If a partner channel were to collect consent on PanEuropean's behalf, the arrangement would be assessed under Document 17 before use.

## **3. Status Relative to Client Relationship and Marketing**

Marketing and Digital Banking own the marketing channels (e-mail, in-app push notifications, the online banking portal and the customer-relationship system); Insurance Distribution owns the PPI channel (app, branch and contact centre at loan origination); Digital Banking owns the biometric log-in feature. The Processing Owners in Section 2 decide on the design of their consent requests and on the configuration of the consent management system (CMS) for their purposes — form, granularity, withdrawal path — with Group DPO advice. The Chief Privacy Officer sets the requirements of this document; the Privacy Operations Lead owns the storage and proof of consent.

The collection, storage, application and withdrawal of consent are implemented through the CMS, a component of PanEuropean's technology stack separate from the customer-relationship system and from the Bank's privacy engineering platform (PEP, illustrative). IT Management operates the CMS. The CMS is the single source of consent status for all consuming systems (marketing tools, the app, the customer-relationship system and PEP, which receives a per-customer permission flag before a transformation — Document 9); the RoPA (Document 21) records each consent-based processing activity, not the status of individual consents. The biometric consent is recorded in the CMS as a status (activated or not); no biometric data are stored there.

## **4. Requirements for the Form and Content of Consent**

* **Marketing.** Requested separately from the account terms, never pre-ticked, never a condition of a product, and granular by channel (e-mail separately from push). The request names PanEuropean as controller, the products concerned, the data used (contact details and product holdings), the channels, and how to withdraw. The interface offers "Yes" and "No" with equal prominence and does not repeat the request after a refusal for at least 12 months (internal policy, EDPB Guidelines 03/2022).

* **Personalised offers.** A separate request in the app naming the purpose (offers selected using contact details and the product preferences in the customer-relationship system), stating that no automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects are made, and how to withdraw.

* **PPI health data.** PPI is optional: a loan is never conditional on PPI, and declining PPI has no effect on the credit decision (Art. 7(4); health data are never an input to creditworthiness assessment). Explicit consent is captured on a separate screen when the insurance application is submitted: the customer confirms an express statement that names the health questionnaire data, the purpose (arranging the insurance and forwarding the application and any claim to the insurer) and the insurer as recipient, and then confirms it in a second step with the app's authentication (two-stage verification). As internal policy — stricter than the legal standard — PanEuropean does not use a single tick box for explicit consent. Applications in a branch or through the contact centre use a paper or electronic form with the same information and a signed statement; staff of Insurance Distribution enter the consent into the CMS before the health data are processed and in any case no later than the next business day.

* **Biometric log-in.** Off by default. On activation, the app shows an express statement that the app's biometric module will create a template stored only on the device and used only to log in, that a PIN or password log-in remains available at all times, and how to switch the feature off; the customer confirms with the existing PIN.

* **Age.** The age is taken from the date of birth verified at account opening (customer due diligence). If a verification error is later discovered and the customer was in fact under 16, the consent is treated as never valid; processing based on it stops at once, the CMS record is annotated, and no consent is sought again until the customer turns 16. The Processing Owner decides on Legal & Compliance's assessment; the Group DPO advises.

* **Changes to the consent text.** Where the marketing text changes materially (for example, a new channel or a new product category), customers who consented under the earlier version are asked again in the app; communications covering the changed scope are not sent until consent under the new version is recorded. Editorial changes are recorded as a new text version in the CMS without a new request.

## **5. Procedure for Recording Consent**

The CMS records: the customer; the purpose (e-mail marketing, push marketing, personalised offers, PPI health data, biometric log-in); the version of the consent text; the date, time and channel; the means of confirmation; and the status. It keeps an append-only history for each consent (obtained → withdrawn or expired → obtained again), so that PanEuropean can demonstrate to the HBDI or another data protection authority — or, in a dispute under national unfair-competition law, to a court — that a specific message was sent while consent was valid, even where consent has since been withdrawn.

Proof of marketing consent is kept for the duration of consent and afterwards as long as needed to demonstrate it, at most three years (Document 11, Section 5; Document 1, Section 11).

## **6. Withdrawal, Opt-Out and Propagation**

* **Marketing and personalised offers.** A single tap on the "Marketing offers" and "Personalised offers" toggles in the app or the online banking portal (the preference centre is an interface to the CMS, not a separate store), with no authentication beyond the standard log-in. The withdrawal is recorded in the CMS and takes effect at once; the same toggle records an objection to direct marketing (Art. 21(2)–(3)). Withdrawals received by telephone, letter or e-mail are recorded by the contact-centre agent directly in the CMS during the interaction (Document 15, Section 5).

* **PPI health data.** The customer withdraws in the app or through the branch or contact centre. The withdrawal is recorded in the CMS at once and processing of the health data by PanEuropean stops; Insurance Distribution then forwards the withdrawal to the insurer and informs the customer that the insurer may be unable to continue the application or claim. The customer is never asked to confirm the withdrawal after hearing the consequences.

* **Biometric log-in.** Switching the feature off in the app ends the consent; the app deletes the template on the device (Document 11, Section 5). PanEuropean holds no server-side template, so there is nothing further to erase at the Bank.

* **Propagation.** The CMS publishes each status change to the marketing tools, the app, the customer-relationship system and PEP within minutes, so the customer is suppressed from marketing selection on the day the withdrawal is received. Dispatch is stopped at once: the delivery path performs a live suppression check against the CMS immediately before each message is sent, and the CMS cancels any queued or scheduled messages for the customer when the withdrawal is recorded, so no message leaves after the withdrawal, whatever the campaign's duration. PanEuropean's e-mail and push delivery service provider (a processor under an Art. 28 contract, Document 17) sends only messages that have passed this check, and deletes the contact data of withdrawn customers within one business day of the daily synchronisation (deletion of retained copies, not the stopping of use). Personalisation segments and the marketing profile of a customer who withdraws are deleted, and the customer is removed from marketing-analytics extracts, off-cycle within 5 business days (illustrative internal deadline; Document 11, Section 3.5) — no batch run extends that deadline. Data retained on another basis are not used for marketing; the proof of consent is kept in the restricted state (Document 11, Section 4.1).

* **Right to object.** The first marketing message under any new purpose includes a clear link to object, separate from the general link to the Customer Privacy Notice (Art. 21(4)).

## **7. Link Between Consent Status and the Permission to Process**

Before every campaign, the marketing platform reads the status in the CMS in real time; customers whose consent has been withdrawn or has expired are excluded regardless of lists held in the marketing tools, which keep a copy of the status only for the duration of the campaign; that copy never overrides the live suppression check performed before each dispatch. The step is recorded on PanEuropean's process map (Document 24, core process C5). Before health data in a PPI application are processed, the application workflow checks the PPI consent status in the CMS; processing does not start, or stops, if consent has been withdrawn, whatever the stage of the application.

## **8. Renewal and Expiry of Consent**

As a policy choice — not a legal requirement — marketing consent and personalisation consent are valid for one year from the date given and may be renewed in the app; the customer is invited to renew 30 days before expiry. If the customer does not renew, consent expires and the customer is excluded from marketing without further notice. PPI health-data consent lasts for the term of the insurance application or policy and is not renewed separately. Biometric consent lasts until the customer switches the feature off.

Account closure: as internal policy, reflecting reasonable expectations, marketing and personalisation consents expire when the customer closes their last account with PanEuropean; the CMS records the status "expired — account closed", and the proof of consent then follows Document 11.

# **International Adaptation**

> **Guidance.** The GDPR is the baseline. The applicability map for Canada is in Document 7, Section 3.1. This box states what changes; it does not claim compliance with any jurisdiction's law.

* **United Kingdom (headline).** The UK GDPR consent standard mirrors the EU text; electronic marketing and cookies are governed by the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR), which the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 amends in phases (for example, new exceptions to the cookie-consent rule) — record which provisions have commenced at each review.
* **Switzerland (headline).** Under the revFADP, consent is not the general basis of lawful processing; where consent is required, it is valid only if given freely, for one or more specific processing operations, after adequate information, and it must be express for sensitive personal data.
* **Canada — PIPEDA.** Consent is the primary rule, subject to the exceptions in the Act, rather than one basis among several. Consent is valid only if it is reasonable to expect that the individual would understand the nature, purpose and consequences of the processing (section 6.1). The OPC *Guidelines for obtaining meaningful consent* expect key elements to be emphasised up front (what information, with whom it is shared, for what purposes, and the risk of significant harm), a layered and user-controlled presentation, express consent where information is sensitive, the use is outside reasonable expectations or there is a meaningful residual risk of significant harm, respect for "no-go zones", and consent treated as an ongoing process. The form of consent follows the sensitivity of the information (Principle 4.3).
* **Canada — Québec (P-39.1).** Consent must be manifest, free and enlightened and given for specific purposes; it is requested for each purpose, in clear and simple terms, and separately from other information where the request is made in writing. Consent to the use or communication of sensitive personal information must be given expressly. Personal information concerning a minor under 14 is not collected from the minor without the consent of the person having parental authority or the tutor, unless collection is clearly for the minor's benefit. Technological products or services offered to the public must provide the highest level of confidentiality by default (Document 16).
* **Canada — CASL (headline).** Commercial electronic messages require express or implied consent (implied consent includes, for a limited period, an existing business relationship), identification of the sender and a working unsubscribe mechanism that is given effect promptly. This is the Canadian counterpart of the ePrivacy marketing rules and of the soft opt-in, not of GDPR consent; record it in Document 7.
